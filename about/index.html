Duality

An interactive piece of art to play with particle-wave duality

The idea

Is light a wave or a particle? Quantum physics answers: both, depending on how you look at it. Duality takes this riddle as a playground. A field of waves and a swarm of particles share the same canvas, and each one keeps shaping the other: particles throw energy into the field, and the ripples they create steer the particles in return.

The piece borrows its inspiration from walking droplets: oil droplets bouncing on a vibrating bath, guided by the very waves they emit. In 2005, Yves Couder and Emmanuel Fort showed that these macroscopic objects reproduce some strikingly quantum-like behaviors, such as diffraction and interference through slits.

Two other families of models from the physics of active matter join the droplets: phoretic particles, which drift along the chemical field they release, and elastoplastic models, where stress slowly builds up and is suddenly released once it crosses a threshold. A single set of equations covers all three, and each mode of the piece tunes it towards one of them.

Technical details

The three families differ by how particles move, how they feed the field, and how the field responds:

Walking droplets
Active particles with a self-propulsion speed \(v_0\) along their orientation \(\vec e(\theta_i)\), which rotates under the field gradient (\(\omega \neq 0\)). They inject small amounts of energy frequently. The medium is inertial: the field oscillates and waves propagate (\(\tau_c\) large enough).
Phoretic particles
Particles move down the field gradient, \(\vec v_{ph} = -\mu \vec\nabla c\), and inject energy into the field continuously. The field is overdamped: no oscillations, it only spreads out.
Elastoplastic model
Particles follow Brownian motion. Injection is rare, triggered by a threshold, and coupled to the local value of the field. The field is overdamped too.

The field

The background is a grid holding a scalar field c(x, y, t). It obeys a damped, non-linear wave equation:

τc ∂²c/∂t² + ∂c/∂t = Dc Δc − ν₁ c − ν₃ c³

  • Dc sets how fast and far waves propagate (diffusion),
  • τc is the characteristic time of the field, its inertia,
  • ν₁ is a viscosity pulling the field back to rest,
  • ν₃ damps large amplitudes and keeps the field from exploding.

Walls are a second field, w(x, y), equal to 1 inside walls and 0 elsewhere. Waves cannot cross them, so they reflect and diffract, and particles are pushed away from them.

Technical details

Time integration

The second-order equation is split into two first-order updates, on the field \(c\) and on its variation \(\dot c\), each with an optional numerical viscosity \(\eta\) (zero by default):

\[ \dot c(t+dt) = (1-\eta_v)\left(1-\frac{dt}{\tau_c}\right)\dot c(t) + \frac{D_c}{\tau_c}\,\Delta c\,dt - \frac{\nu_1}{\tau_c}\,c\,dt - \frac{\nu_3}{\tau_c}\,c^3\,dt \] \[ c(t+dt) = (1-\eta_c\,dt)\,c(t) + \dot c(t+dt)\,dt \]

Values are then clamped to \([-1, 1]\) to protect against explosions. The grid is periodic: what leaves on one side comes back on the other.

Physics runs at a fixed step of 12 ms of machine time, \(dt' = dt \times 12\) with \(dt = 0.1\), independently of the frame rate. A slow device runs several steps per frame (up to 8) to keep time linear, and the scheme stays below its stability threshold on any device.

Waves or spreading

Whether the field oscillates or only spreads out depends on its inertia \(\tau_c\). Neglecting the viscosities, a mode of wavenumber \(q\), \(c \propto e^{i\vec q \cdot \vec r + st}\), satisfies \(\tau_c s^2 + s + D_c q^2 = 0\). It oscillates, and waves propagate, when the roots are complex:

\[ \tau_c > \frac{1}{4\,q^2 D_c} \]

Here \(D_c q^2\) is the rate at which that mode would relax by pure diffusion. Above this threshold the medium is inertial, like the bath of the walking droplets. Below it, the field is overdamped, as in the phoretic and elastoplastic models.

Discrete Laplacian

On the grid, the Laplacian is estimated from a set of neighbouring cells \(\vec{dr}_k\):

\[ \Delta c(\vec r) = \frac{2 \sum_k \left[c(\vec r + \vec{dr}_k) - c(\vec r)\right]}{\sum_k \|\vec{dr}_k\|^2}, \qquad \vec{dr}_k = r_0\,\vec{dr}_k^{\,cell} \]

where \(r_0\) is the cell-to-reality scale factor. The code supports three neighbourhoods: von Neumann 1 (4 adjacent cells), hollow von Neumann 2 and Moore 1 (the 8 surrounding cells, used for the field).

Coupling with walls

Each term of the Laplacian is weighted by how free both cells are from walls:

\[ \Delta c(\vec r) = \frac{2}{\sum_k \|\vec{dr}_k\|^2} \sum_k \left(1 - \tfrac12\left[w(\vec r + \vec{dr}_k) + w(\vec r)\right]\right)\left[c(\vec r + \vec{dr}_k) - c(\vec r)\right] \]

Between two free cells the usual Laplacian is recovered, and nothing flows into a wall. Inside walls, the field is held at zero.

The particles

Each particle has a position r, an orientation θ and an internal energy u. They behave like active Brownian particles:

dr/dt = v₀ e(θ) − µ ∇c − σ ∇w + √(2Dp) ξ

dθ/dt = ω (cos θ ∂yc − sin θ ∂xc) + √(2Dr) ξ

du/dt = 1/τu + λ |c|

  • v₀ is a self-propulsion speed along the orientation,
  • µ makes particles slide down the field gradient,
  • σ sets how strongly walls push particles away,
  • ω makes them turn according to the gradient,
  • ξ is random noise (Brownian motion, in position and rotation).

Energy builds up over time, and faster where the field is strong. Once it crosses a threshold, the particle releases all of it into the field around it, creating a new ripple, and starts charging again. This is the bounce of the droplet: the particle writes the wave, the wave guides the particle.

Technical details

Update rules

Each particle \(i\) is advanced with an Euler–Maruyama scheme, where \(\partial_x c\) and \(\partial_y c\) are the field gradient components at the particle's cell:

\[ \theta_i(t+dt) = \theta_i(t) + \omega\left(\cos\theta_i\,\partial_y c - \sin\theta_i\,\partial_x c\right)dt + \sqrt{2D_r\,dt}\;\xi_{\theta_i} \] \[ \vec v_i(t) = v_0\,\vec e(\theta_i) - \mu\,\vec\nabla c - \sigma\,\vec\nabla w \] \[ x_i(t+dt) = x_i(t) + v_{x_i}(t)\,dt + \sqrt{2D_p\,dt}\;\xi_{x_i} \] \[ y_i(t+dt) = y_i(t) + v_{y_i}(t)\,dt + \sqrt{2D_p\,dt}\;\xi_{y_i} \] \[ u_i(t+dt) = u_i(t) + \frac{dt}{\tau_u} + \lambda\,\left|c(x_i, y_i)\right|dt \]

The rotation term is the vertical component of \(\omega\,\vec e(\theta_i) \times \vec\nabla c\): particles turn towards or away from the gradient depending on the sign of \(\omega\). The noises \(\xi_{\theta_i}, \xi_{x_i}, \xi_{y_i}\) follow a normal distribution \(\mathcal N(0, 1)\), drawn with the Box–Muller transform.

Energy release

When \(u_i(t+dt)\) exceeds the particle's threshold \(s_i\), the energy is injected into the field, shared equally between the neighbouring cells, and \(u_i\) is reset to zero. A new threshold is then drawn:

\[ s_i = \alpha\,\left(-\ln U\right) + (1-\alpha), \qquad U \sim \mathcal U(0, 1] \]

The stochasticity \(\alpha \in [0, 1]\) moves the threshold from a fixed value of 1 (\(\alpha = 0\)) to an exponentially distributed random value (\(\alpha = 1\)).

Walls

Walls act on particles through a potential proportional to the wall field:

\[ \phi(x, y) = \sigma\,w(x, y) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec F = -\vec\nabla\phi = -\sigma\,\vec\nabla w \]

This force is added to the particle velocity. Particles that still end up inside a wall get their self-propulsion boosted so they find their way out.

The sound

The field is also an instrument. Depending on the mode, the grid is read as a raw audio signal, analysed through its frequency spectrum, or voiced by the particles themselves. What you hear is the same matter as what you see.

Technical details

Four sound layers are mixed differently in each mode:

Raw grid
The field values are played directly as a waveform. An audio worklet scans the grid cell after cell at 44.1 kHz, with about 17.6 samples per cell and linear interpolation between cells.
Spectrum
The whole grid is read as one signal and analysed with a 256-point FFT. A bank of 32 sine oscillators, tuned to a C minor pentatonic scale across octaves, takes its loudness from the matching frequency bins.
Field tone
A sine oscillator whose pitch rises with the maximum amplitude of the field, \(f = 20 + \sqrt{500\,\max|c|}\) Hz.
Particles
A square wave whose pitch follows the total speed of the particles.

Grid layers go through a 50 Hz high-pass and a 3 kHz low-pass filter.

The modes

The Playground
Two thousand particles and a field to stir with your pointer. Tune energy injection and diffusion.
The Tempest
Raindrops fall at random on the surface. Play with their intensity and frequency.
The Constellations
A dense swarm in which particles draw links to nearby partners, like stars in the night sky.
The Bouncing Droplets
A handful of self-propelled droplets, each surfing on its own wave and on the waves of the others.
The Interferences
A double-slit experiment: waves pass through two openings and interfere on the other side.
The Poem
Words are written into the field, letter by letter, and particles gather around their shapes.

Notes and code: where they differ

The equations above follow the code. The original handwritten notes of the model differ from it on a few points:

  1. To discuss Energy coupling. The notes charge a particle with \(\lambda\,c\), the code with \(\lambda\,|c|\). With the absolute value, a particle also gains energy in the troughs of the field, so it charges up wherever the field is agitated, not only where it is positive.
  2. Non-critical Threshold law. The notes write the new threshold as \(\alpha + (1-\alpha)(-\ln U)\), the code as \(\alpha(-\ln U) + (1-\alpha)\). The role of \(\alpha\) is simply mirrored: in the code, \(\alpha = 0\) gives a fixed threshold and \(\alpha = 1\) a fully random one.
  3. Non-critical Wall smoothing. The notes smooth the wall field iteratively into a staircase going from 1 inside walls to 0 outside. In the code, \(w\) is strictly 0 or 1, so the wall force only acts at the edge of walls.
  4. Adaptive performance Machine time. The notes scale each step by the frame duration, \(dt' = dt \times \Delta T_{image}\), so that time flows linearly whatever the frame rate. The code now uses a fixed physics step of 12 ms instead, and runs as many steps per frame as the elapsed time requires. The physics remains coherent:
    • Every step applies exactly the same discrete equations, so the dynamics no longer depend on the device. A variable \(dt'\) could exceed the stability limit of the scheme on a slow device.
    • When a device cannot keep up, even with 8 steps per frame, the backlog is dropped: the simulation runs slower than real time, but it stays correct in simulation time.
    • At startup, slow devices get a coarser grid and fewer particles. Cells become larger, but the Laplacian is computed in screen units (\(r_0\) is the cell size), so \(D_c\) keeps the same meaning and waves travel the same screen distances, with less detail.
    • The injection of each particle is divided by \(\sqrt{N/10}\) for a population \(N\), so with fewer particles the total energy fed into the field decreases as \(\sqrt N\), not as \(N\). The light profiles look somewhat calmer, but they are not identical to the full simulation.

The code and AI

Duality began as a prototype on CodePen and grew through three stages. The switch to AI assistance was progressive, with its share growing along the way.

  1. Spring 2025

    The ideas

    The models and the first sketches of equations, on paper and whiteboards.

  2. From November 2025

    By hand

    Written with no AI assistance, until the simulation equations were fully coded, the audio base could drive simple oscillators and the keyboard controls were in place. The JavaScript then counted about 850 lines.

  3. From April 2026

    By hand, with Gemini

    Still written by hand, with Gemini assisting on the sinusoidal orchestra, on adapting performance to devices of different computing power, and on gamepad controls. The code reached about 1500 lines.

  4. From September 2026

    With Copilot and Claude

    Copilot and Claude helped add more modes, improve the interface, build the guide overlay, and write this page from handwritten notes listing all the models precisely, gathered in a PDF. The code now counts about 2150 lines.

The art

« J’ai rêvé d’être une onde », s’écrie la particule
Alors qu’en elle s’éveille la partie qui ondule

“I dreamt of being a wave,” cries the particle,
As within it awakens the part that ripples.

Duality was born in spring 2025 as a co-creation between Eric Bertin and Alex Andrix, started with no idea of what form it would take. Only a question: which dualities? Rigour and creativity, man and woman, wave and particle. And beneath them, the very concept of duality.

From a classical point of view, waves and particles are well defined. Quantum objects behave dually, with the properties of both, but the theory says nothing about how to picture the system between two measurements. Rather than dwell on that mystery, we chose to stay on the classical side, with no ambiguity about whether things exist. Bouncing droplets are such an object: a composite that is both wave and particle at once. No ontological mystery here, both simply exist at the same time.

So we play with classical models: a single physical object, a wave propagating on a network of cells, waves turned into sound and into music. And with two questions, asked from the inside:

I am a particle: how does it show in the world around me?
I am a wave: how does it show in the world around me?

We did not force ourselves to carry the logic all the way, to the point of using the same mathematical model as both artistic and scientific source. The equations used in the project have been defined by Eric Bertin on a whiteboard and coded by Alex Andrix. As such, they are grounded in real physics, but the parameters are chosen for what they show and what they sound like. The work went back and forth like a game of ping-pong, between theory and simulated play, with the pleasure of exploring as its compass.

“Saying I is at the heart of the artistic process.”

Eric Bertin

Am I a particle? Am I a wave? Have fun finding out, and be careful with your precious ears: keep the sound low.

Eric Bertin & Alex Andrix
